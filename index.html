Methodology — How the Segments Were Built

From raw transaction logs to a validated 3-segment model, so the numbers above can be trusted

The source data is a raw UK online-retail transaction export — one row per line item, not per customer. Before any modeling happened, it had to be cleaned and re-shaped into one behavioral profile per customer. The pipeline below is what turns 38,209 transaction rows into the 909-customer, 13-feature table everything else in this report is built on.

Why nearly a third of rows were dropped: segmentation is done at the customer level, so any line item without a CustomerID (guest checkouts / unlinked orders) can't be attributed to a behavioral profile and was excluded, along with rows with a zero/negative unit price or zero quantity. This is a standard, defensible cut for RFM-style modeling — but it's worth flagging to stakeholders that roughly 1 in 3 raw transactions could not be tied to a known customer.

Zero Quantity (returns/adjustments) included above— Dropped — zero-quantity rows contribute no purchase signal
Missing Country 1 row<0.1% Kept — irrelevant to the 13 behavioral features used
✅

Net result: 25,033 of 38,209 raw rows retained (65.5%), covering 909 unique, fully-attributable customers. The only material loss is the missing-CustomerID gap — worth a separate conversation with the checkout/CRM team about capturing customer identity more consistently at the point of sale, independent of this segmentation project.

Choosing k — Silhouette Score by Cluster Count

Tested k = 2 through 10 on the scaled 13-feature space

PCA Variance Explained (visualization only)

Clustering used all 13 scaled features — PCA is for the 2D/3D views only
⚖️

k = 2 scored higher (0.583) — so why did we choose k = 3 (0.432)? The two-cluster split is statistically the cleanest, but it collapses into a single "everyone else" group with no way to separate healthy regular customers from the near-zero-value At Risk cohort. k = 3 gives up some statistical tightness for a segmentation that's actually actionable: three groups map directly to three different business responses (protect, grow, investigate). Silhouette scores for k = 4–10 all drop below 0.41, confirming 3 is the best available balance of separation and usability, not an arbitrary choice.

🔧

Feature prep: all 13 features (Recency, Frequency, Monetary, Total Quantity, Unique Products, Average Order Value, Average Quantity per Order, Average Unit Price, Customer Lifetime Days, Average Days Between Orders, Purchase Rate, Cancellation Rate, Weekend Purchase Ratio) were log-transformed to tame the heavy right-skew typical of spend data, then standardized before KMeans. The first two principal components explain 67.4% of total variance (PC1: 43.3%, PC2: 24.1%) — enough for the 2D/3D visuals in this report to be a faithful picture of the underlying separation.

The Hidden Truth — Customer Count ≠ Revenue

Weighting each segment's average spend by its size tells a completely different story

The At Risk segment isn't just "at risk" — it's functionally non-revenue. Its average monetary value per customer is $0.20, driven by a 99.47% order cancellation rate — essentially every transaction this group initiates gets cancelled. Meanwhile, High Value customers are only a quarter of the base but generate 54.7% of estimated total revenue. Regular customers are the majority by headcount but punch below their weight on monetary contribution.

99.47%
Cancellation rate in the At Risk segment — 63 customers (6.9% of the base) whose orders almost never convert into real revenue. This group should be treated as a churn / data-quality flag, not a marketing target.

Roadmap — From Insight to Action

Prioritized recommendations and projected impact

+$150K+
Estimated annual revenue upside from combining Regular→High Value nurture campaigns with eliminating At Risk-segment waste — without new customer acquisition spend.
🚨

Investigate the At Risk Segment — It May Not Be a Marketing Problem

A 99.47% cancellation rate is not typical "at risk" churn behavior — it looks more like payment failures, bot/fraud activity, or a checkout bug affecting this cohort specifically.

  • Audit payment gateway logs for this segment's transactions
  • Check for shared IP/device fingerprints (fraud signal)
  • Exclude this group from retention/win-back campaigns until root cause is found
⚠️

Protect and Expand the High Value Segment

227 customers generate 54.7% of revenue on a 3.51% cancellation rate — the healthiest segment by far. Losing even a handful of these customers has an outsized financial impact.

  • Set up a VIP/loyalty tier with white-glove support
  • Proactive outreach if recency drifts upward (early churn signal)
  • Use their 36 unique-product average to power cross-sell recommendations
📈

Build a Regular → High Value Nurture Track

619 Regular customers are the largest pool of upside. A targeted uplift campaign (bundle offers, loyalty points, personalized recommendations) could shift a meaningful share toward High Value behavior.

  • Target customers already above-median on Frequency within Regular
  • Incentivize a 3rd/4th order with free-shipping thresholds
  • Track segment migration monthly to measure campaign lift
🔁

Re-run Segmentation Quarterly

Silhouette score of 0.43 is solid but not perfect — customer behavior shifts over time, especially around holidays. Refresh the model regularly to keep segment boundaries and campaigns accurate.

  • Re-cluster quarterly with the same 13 features for consistency
  • Track segment size drift as an early business-health indicator
  • Consider testing k=4 to see if Regular splits into finer sub-groups
